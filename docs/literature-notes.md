# Literature notes

Full-text reading of the 22 papers in `references/` (git-ignored), 2026-09-19. Three parts:

- **Part A** — per-paper interpretation: main point, relevance, what we learn, gaps.
- **Part B** — content to carry into the proposal's prior-work section, by theme, with a synthesis of how the papers interact.
- **Part C** — the summary: three paragraphs, at most two papers each.

## Method

1. From the abstract-level knowledge we had, 1–3 content-specific questions were written per paper under each of four headings (main point / relevance / what we learn / gaps) — 22 papers, ~130 questions.
2. Three readers (separate Claude agents, no shared context) answered every question from the PDF text only, citing tables and sections.
3. The three answer sets were compared line by line. They agreed on every number and every quoted claim; the only differences were emphasis. Where a paper contradicts itself, all three flagged the same spot. Key numbers were then re-checked against the text.

**Corrections to what we believed before reading in full** (all three readers found the same):

| Belief before | What the paper actually says |
|---|---|
| Helmke 2022: "80% detection at 11% false alarms" | That is the scripted proof-of-concept. On **real ops-room recordings** the combined system had 82% detection at a **67% false-alarm rate** (Table V). |
| AIRHILT uses an LLM to reason about conflicts | The LLM (GPT-OSS-20B) is **NLG-only** — it rewords advisory text. Conflict detection is a deterministic rule ladder. |
| Ghazanfari 2026 evaluates LLM *interpretation* of ATC instructions | The LLM **plays the controller** and generates replies. It is a generation study; the transferable finding is error accumulation over turns. |
| "Wang et al. 2024" review; "Jones 2007" NASA report | Single-author Liu 2024; Young & Jones 2001. Citations fixed in `docs/references.md`. |
| SCOPE's rule baseline shows rules fail at readback checking | SCOPE's "rules" are character-overlap heuristics (25% accuracy). Helmke's rule-based state machine — a real rule system — **beat** their data-driven extractor and reached 83% detection. "Rules" means very different things in the two papers. |
| Papers 05 and 06 are two Rovira & Parasuraman papers | Byte-identical PDF. One paper. |

## Part B — Content to carry into the prior-work section

Each section: the candidate papers, what each contributes (research → what we learn → gaps), then a synthesis of how they interact. Selection criteria: relevance to a voice-derived runway-clearance checker; says something we care about; tells us what to do or what to watch for; and answers what the milestone asks — what is known, what is contested, and how to evaluate.

### B1. Current systems and why the gap exists

**Candidates considered:** Weiß 2013, Young & Jones 2001, Helmke 2022, Ruskin 2021, (Zuluaga-Gomez 2023 for corpus context). Combinations tried: {Weiß, Young & Jones} covers strips and surveillance but says nothing about voice; {Weiß, Helmke} contrasts structured input with voice input and is the sharpest; {Weiß, Helmke, Young & Jones} adds why surveillance is not a substitute. The last is used.

**Weiß et al. 2013.** *Research:* DFS prototype that turns strip-entered clearances into cleared runway segments and flags any two mobiles cleared for the same segment. *Learn:* the conflict taxonomy (all pairs of LUP/CRS/TOF/LND, with the same-direction exemption) is a validated specification for a runway ledger; conditional clearances should not count until their condition is met; the authors themselves expect the main practical problem to be **warnings about situations controllers consider safe**. *Gaps:* never evaluated — no recall, no false-alarm rate; input is a controller's typed entry, so extraction error is a failure mode they never face; time is abstracted away, so expiry has to come from surveillance.

**Helmke et al. 2022.** *Research:* the only system that derives clearances from **voice** and checks them, on real recordings. *Learn:* on 7.7 h of real audio, 82% of the 87 real readback errors were detected at a 67% false-alarm rate; the same system on scripted trials scored 82% / 13%. The ladder gold-annotation → gold-transcription → ASR (FAR 18 → 58 → 67%) locates most of the damage in **text-to-concept extraction**, not ASR. Rules beat the T5 extractor and are "easier to certify". At 2% base rate, 85% accuracy implies an 88% false-alarm rate — the arithmetic every readback-checking result has to survive. *Gaps:* en-route, single instruction–readback pairs; no cross-aircraft state; conditional clearances fail; whether 67% is acceptable is deferred to human-in-the-loop trials.

**Young & Jones 2001.** *Research:* NASA flight tests of surveillance-based incursion alerting. *Learn:* airborne alerting worked (43–44 of 47) but every miss and false alert came from inaccurate or late traffic data; ground-based alerting missed 13 of 47. Incursions are ~5 per million operations, so any monitoring task "naturally lends itself to complacency". *Gaps:* prototypes; nothing on clearance content. (Deployment today: ASDE-X/ASSC at 44 US airports, no expansion planned — DOT OIG 2025, see `docs/incidents/`.)

**Synthesis.** Two lines of defence exist against conflicting runway clearances, and both have a hole the other cannot fill. Surveillance-based alerting (Young & Jones; ASDE-X) watches where aircraft *are*, but is only as good as its data and is installed at a minority of towers. Clearance cross-checking (Weiß) watches what the controller *said* — earlier, and independent of surveillance — but only where the clearance has been typed into a strip system, and it has never been evaluated. Helmke is the bridge: the one system that takes clearances from the voice channel, and its real-audio result (82% / 67%) is the realistic ceiling for anything built on speech. The three papers build on each other rather than disagree: Weiß gives the logic, Helmke gives the input path and the cost of that path, Young & Jones give the reason a voice-derived check is worth having at all. The gap is exact: nobody has combined Weiß's runway ledger with Helmke's voice input and measured recall and false alarms on real tower audio. That is what we do — and Helmke's ladder tells us in advance where to look for the damage.

### B2. LLM-based attempts and what is contested

**Candidates considered:** Chang 2026, Deng 2026 (SCOPE), Ghazanfari 2026, Helmke 2022, Sangeetha 2025, Darrell 2026, AIRHILT. Combinations tried: {SCOPE, Chang} gives the cleanest contradiction; adding Helmke shows what "rules" mean and that the same data-realism gap (scripted vs real) appears in non-LLM work; adding Ghazanfari explains *why* the LLM should not hold state; Sangeetha as a foil (LLMs tolerate WER — for coarse labels). Used: Chang, SCOPE, Helmke, Ghazanfari.

**Deng et al. 2026 (SCOPE).** *Research:* frozen LLM plus a small classifier, 91% accuracy detecting anomalous readbacks. *Learn:* the plug-in classifier carries the result (−17 points without it); the LLM's contribution is fixing the "Incorrect" class. Their test set is 52% anomalous; the "rule-based" baselines at 25% are character-overlap heuristics. *Gaps:* anomalies are synthesised from correct readbacks; no ASR; no false-alarm rate at operational prevalence; no real-operational validation.

**Chang et al. 2026.** *Research:* 1,000 real Changi ground-frequency utterances, zero-shot frontier LLMs, consequence-weighted Risk Score with weights from five tower controllers. *Learn:* macro-F1 up to 0.86 hides a Risk Score of at most 0.69; runway extraction is "particularly fragile"; with Whisper input the score falls from 0.45 to 0.04–0.07 and the strict score to zero. Controllers weighted callsign 1.00 and runway 0.95. *Gaps:* ground, not tower; raw text only — no dialogue context, no rule baseline, no over-extraction penalty.

**Helmke et al. 2022** (as the non-LLM comparison). *Learn:* a rule state machine detected 83% of real readback errors alone; the data-driven confirmer added nothing to detection and reduced false alarms only from 69% to 67%; rules beat T5 at extraction. Their scripted-trial vs ops-room gap (13% vs 67% FAR) is the same data-realism gap that separates SCOPE from Chang.

**Ghazanfari et al. 2026.** *Research:* LLMs generating controller replies over a real 36-turn flight. *Learn:* once an early answer is wrong it "enters its context and makes the next … more likely" — error accumulation is "a major source of failure"; models "sound like controllers without making correct decisions". *Gaps:* generation, not extraction; single flight.

**Synthesis.** The literature contains a direct contradiction and it is not about model quality. SCOPE reports an LLM beating rules by 65 points; Chang reports frontier LLMs failing on the one slot that matters most. The difference is the data: SCOPE evaluates on real transcripts with *synthesised* anomalies and a balanced test set; Chang evaluates on *real* utterances, on real ASR output, with consequence weighting. Helmke shows the same split inside a non-LLM system — 13% false alarms on scripted trials, 67% on real audio — so the pattern is general: scripted or balanced data flatter any detector, and the honest number only appears with real prevalence and real noise. The contradiction also turns on what "rules" means: SCOPE's rules are string overlap; Helmke's are a typed state machine that outperformed a transformer. Our rules parser is of the second kind, so SCOPE's 25% is not our baseline's ceiling. Ghazanfari adds the architectural lesson: an LLM that carries its own state drifts, which is the empirical case for keeping the LLM to extraction and the state in a deterministic engine. What is *contested*, then, is whether LLM extraction survives real ASR noise and real base rates. Chang says no, with weights that put runway near the top; SCOPE says yes, on data where the question cannot arise. Our design answers it on real tower transcripts with human gold, under the exact ladder Helmke used.

### B3. Human factors of alerting

**Candidates considered:** Wickens 2009, Ruskin 2021, Parasuraman & Manzey 2010, Rovira & Parasuraman 2010, Trapsilawati 2016. Combinations tried: {Wickens, Ruskin} is the clean disagreement; {Parasuraman & Manzey, Rovira & Parasuraman} is the mechanism and its controlled demonstration; Trapsilawati is the optimistic outlier. Used: Wickens, Ruskin, Rovira & Parasuraman, Parasuraman & Manzey; Trapsilawati as one sentence.

**Wickens et al. 2009.** *Research:* 495 real en-route alerts, 45–47% false. *Learn:* no cry-wolf effect — 1.5% non-response to true alerts, no slowing in high-false-alarm centres; ~80% of false alerts were "forgivable" because the threshold was conservative, the alert served the controller's own task, and it was visual rather than aural. Their false-alarm rate is 1 − precision, computed per alert because non-alert events have no denominator. *Gaps:* en-route only; the authors warn against generalising to terminal airspace.

**Ruskin et al. 2021.** *Research:* 370 ASRS reports from controllers. *Learn:* only 16% of reported signals were false alarms, yet controllers describe ignoring, muting and suspending alarms; tower reports call ASDE-X "a constant problem" and one describes false ASDE-X alarms reducing response to *other* alarms. They separate true false alarms from **nuisance alarms** (correct, but no action needed) and recommend alarms that say why they fired, how likely the condition is, and how urgent. *Gaps:* voluntary reports, no rates.

**Rovira & Parasuraman 2010.** *Research:* 12 professional controllers, a conflict probe with one miss or one false alarm. *Learn:* detection on the failure event fell from 100% to 25% (miss) and 50% (false alarm); in the false-alarm condition half the controllers ignored a true conflict displayed at the same time and looked *least* at the conflicting aircraft. Recommends tools to inspect the data behind an alert and feedback on when the automation fails. *Gaps:* no voice, single failure, low power.

**Parasuraman & Manzey 2010.** *Research:* integrative review. *Learn:* complacency and automation bias persist in experts and are not trained away; **more reliable automation produces worse monitoring** (33% vs 82% failure detection under constant vs variable reliability); alert-only aids cause omission errors; per-alert confidence display and experienced failures during training are the countermeasures that work. *Gaps:* laboratory evidence with inflated failure rates.

**Trapsilawati et al. 2016** — an 80%-reliable aid still doubled conflicts resolved, and trust fell after a failure while dependence did not; but the failure was a miss, never a false alert.

**Synthesis.** The field data disagree, and the disagreement is informative. Wickens found no cry-wolf effect at 47% false alerts; Ruskin found controllers reporting exactly that behaviour at a reported 16%. The reconciliation is in the settings: Wickens' alerts were visual, en-route, on the controller's primary task, and mostly "forgivable" near-misses of the threshold; Ruskin's tower alarms are aural, interrupt a visual task, and — for ASDE-X — are sometimes plainly wrong. Our system is a tower system with a mandatory-response flavour, so Ruskin's is the closer precedent, and Ruskin's nuisance-vs-false distinction is the one our alert adjudication must make: an alert on a legal anticipated-separation takeoff is *correct and unwanted*, which is a different problem from a parser error. Rovira & Parasuraman then show what a false alert costs even when the system is otherwise reliable — half the controllers missed a real conflict shown beside it — and Parasuraman & Manzey supply the paradox: the *better* our engine gets, the less controllers will check it, so a rare miss becomes more dangerous, not less. The four papers do not contradict each other so much as bound the problem: false alarms are survivable under conditions we do not have (Wickens), harmful under conditions we do (Ruskin, Rovira), and even a near-perfect tool changes behaviour (Parasuraman & Manzey). Trapsilawati is the reminder that imperfect aids can still be net positive — which is why the false-alarm rate we measure has to be reported as a *finding about the design*, alongside recall, not tuned away.

### B4. Normative frameworks (for §5 of the proposal, not a prior-work section)

Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven 2018 give the two conditions we use: **tracking** — the system responds to the relevant facts and reasons (for us: alerts when and only when clearances conflict, i.e. recall and false-alarm rate) — and **tracing** — some human understands the system's capabilities and limits and owns its outcomes. Matthias 2004 places rule systems ("can always be inspected and corrected") on the no-gap side and learned systems on the other, which is the line between our engine and the LLM extractor; his own medical example endorses tuning toward false positives when the limits are known. Santoni de Sio & Mecacci 2021 warn that explainability is neither necessary nor sufficient — knowing the tool's *limits* closes gaps as much as seeing its logic — so the report must state the engine's limits (voice-only, no positions, staleness) as prominently as its rules. Cavalcante Siebert 2022 turn this into checks: an explicit operational design domain (tower, runway clearances, standard phraseology), logged decisions (`docs/superpowers/`), and alert timing as a control property.

## Part C — Summary (three paragraphs, at most two papers each)

**Current systems and why the gap exists.** Cross-checking runway clearances for conflicts is an established idea: the DFS system of Weiß et al. (2013) maps clearances to runway segments and flags two mobiles cleared for the same one — but it reads the clearances from electronic flight strips, was never evaluated, and its authors expected its main problem to be warnings about situations controllers consider safe. The one system that takes clearances from the voice channel is Helmke et al. (2022): on 7.7 hours of real en-route audio it detected 82% of readback errors at a 67% false-alarm rate, against 13% on scripted trials, with most of the false alarms traced to semantic extraction rather than speech recognition. Surveillance-based alerting exists but sits at 44 US airports with no expansion planned. No one has combined a runway ledger with voice-derived clearances and measured it on real tower audio; Helmke's numbers are the ceiling to beat and his gold-annotation → gold-transcript → ASR ladder is the way to measure it.

**LLM-based attempts and what is contested.** Two recent papers give opposite answers. SCOPE (Deng et al. 2026) reports an LLM detecting anomalous readbacks at 91% accuracy, against 25% for rules — but its anomalies are synthesised from correct readbacks, its test set is half anomalous where operations run at ~2%, its inputs are clean transcripts, and its "rules" are string overlap. Chang et al. (2026), on 1,000 real Changi utterances with a consequence-weighted score from five tower controllers, find frontier LLMs reach macro-F1 0.86 yet score at most 0.69 on the weighted metric, fail most on runway identifiers, and collapse to near zero on Whisper output. The disagreement is about data realism, not models, and the same scripted-versus-real gap appears inside Helmke's non-LLM system. What is contested is whether LLM extraction survives real ASR noise and real base rates — and the answer is only visible under the conditions Chang used. We adopt Chang's weighting, add the rule baseline Chang lacks, and test on tower transcripts where runway clearances are frequent rather than 1.4% of tokens.

**Human factors of alerting.** The field evidence disagrees. Wickens et al. (2009) analysed 495 real en-route conflict alerts, 47% false, and found no cry-wolf effect: 1.5% non-response to true alerts and no slowing in high-false-alarm centres — because the alerts were visual, served the controller's own task, and mostly missed the threshold narrowly. Ruskin et al. (2021), from 370 controller safety reports, found controllers muting and ignoring alarms at a reported 16% false-alarm share, with tower ASDE-X alarms "a constant problem" and one false-prone alarm eroding trust in others. The settings explain the split: our tool is a tower system closer to Ruskin's, and Ruskin's distinction between a false alarm and a nuisance alarm — correct, but no action needed — is precisely the one an anticipated-separation alert falls into. The wider literature adds that a single false alert can make half of professional controllers miss a real conflict shown beside it, and that more reliable automation is monitored *less*. So the false-alarm rate we measure is not a tuning parameter; it is a finding about whether the tool can help at all, and it is reported as such.
